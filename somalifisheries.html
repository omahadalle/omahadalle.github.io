Fisheries in Somalia: the bigger picture

Somalia has the longest coastline in mainland Africa, yet its fisheries remain largely unstudied. Today's problems make more sense when you follow how the sector has changed across six eras.

Six eras at a glance

Each band shows how long an era lasted. Hover or tap a band for details, or choose an era to jump to it.

    The striped band is not to scale: Somali coastal communities fished for centuries before colonial rule. Era boundaries follow major political turning points; the civil war band ends in 2012, when the Federal Government was formed.

    1Coastal livelihoods before colonial rule

    Pre-colonial · before the 1880s

    For centuries, Somali coastal communities fished the Gulf of Aden and the Indian Ocean from small boats, while Benadir ports such as Mogadishu, Merca and Barawa traded across the Indian Ocean world. Fishing was small-scale and closely tied to local diets and trade, alongside pastoralism inland.

    2Colonial rule and early commercial fishing

    Colonial · 1880s–1960

    Britain and Italy divided the Somali coast into British Somaliland in the north and Italian Somaliland in the south. Fishing remained largely small-scale and subsistence, part of an economy built on farming, herding and trade.

    • 1880sBritain and Italy establish protectorates along the Somali coast.
    • 1950Italian Somaliland becomes a UN Trust Territory under Italian administration, until 1960.

    3Independence and the civilian years

    Independence · 1960–1969

    The new Somali Republic inherited a fisheries sector that was small and poorly documented, with most economic attention on livestock and banana exports.

    • 1960British Somaliland (26 June) and the Trust Territory (1 July) gain independence and unite as the Somali Republic.

    4State-led fisheries under military rule

    Military government · 1969–1991

    The military government made fisheries part of its development plans. After the 1974–75 drought, about 90,000 nomads were resettled into farming and fishing cooperatives. The cooperatives struggled to become profitable, and fishing remained under 1 percent of GDP in the early 1970s, despite an estimated potential yield of 150,000 tons a year.

    • 1969Military coup brings Siad Barre to power.
    • c. 1970Government builds a fish cannery and pier at Laas Qoray.
    • 1974–75Severe drought; about 90,000 nomads are resettled into farming and fishing cooperatives.
    • 1975Fishing cooperatives set up at Eyl, Cadale and Baraawe. Laas Qoray produces 2,220 tons of canned fish.
    • 1978Soviet personnel leave; Australia and Italy take over support for fishing projects.
    • 1979Laas Qoray's canned fish output falls to negligible levels.

    Sources: Economic history of Somalia; Las Khorey. Replace with primary sources as you collect them.

    5State collapse and unguarded waters

    Civil war · 1991–2012

    With no functioning central government, Somalia's waters went largely unmonitored. Foreign vessels fished with little oversight, state fishing infrastructure such as the Laas Qoray cannery shut down, and coastal communities lost both income and protection.

    • 1991Central government collapses and civil war spreads.
    • 2004The Indian Ocean tsunami strikes Somalia's coast.
    • Late 2000sPiracy surges off Somalia, peaking around 2011.

    6Rebuilding fisheries governance

    Post-conflict · 2012–present

    Since the Federal Government formed, Somalia has begun to claim and manage its waters: declaring an exclusive economic zone, revising its fisheries law, and licensing foreign tuna vessels for a fee. Enforcement and data collection remain major gaps.

    • 2012The Federal Government of Somalia is formed.
    • 2014Somalia proclaims a 200-nautical-mile exclusive economic zone (30 June) and revises its fisheries law.
    • 2018First fishing licences issued: 31 Chinese tuna longliners, for first-year fees of US$1,045,000.

    Sources: EEZ proclamation, 30 June 2014 (FAOLEX); World Bank, 2019.
